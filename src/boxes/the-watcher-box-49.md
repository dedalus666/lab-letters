The Watcher - Box 49

Anselm kept the light at the end of a long, narrow point of rock where the sea came in from three sides. For forty years he climbed the same two hundred steps each evening and lit the lamp, and each morning he climbed them again and put it out. Between those two ascents, he observed.

He watched the weather build. He watched ships appear as dark lines on the horizon and disappear as pinpricks of light. He watched the tide lash at the rock in a slow, steady rhythm that never repeated itself. He rarely slept more than three hours at a time, and when he did, in his dreams he was still listening to the crashing waves. 

His daughter Wren grew up in the lamp room. She learned to read the sky before she could read a book. She learned that a watcher’s work is mostly waiting, and that waiting is not empty. It’s a way of caring for something you can’t touch.

When Anselm grew too old for the stairs, Wren took the lamp. He moved to a chair by the window, and he watched her light the lamp from below, until one winter night when he closed his tired eyes forever. 

She wanted to remember him with something that would keep on watching in his place. So she took the old doorframe from the lamp room, scorched on one side by a fire in the year of the great storm, and cut it into a box. She left the scorching as it was: long dark stripes through the grain, like light thrown across moving water. She stained the lid red at its edge, the color of the lamp’s warning glass, and set a diamond in teal at its center, with a smaller red diamond inside it, round and bright as an eye that never closes. She added two wooden handles so the box could be carried to the window or the stairs or the rock, a hinge, and a brass latch to hold it shut.

Inside she put the things he’d carried in his pockets all those years: a worn brass key, a silver coin, a pocket compass, a letter from her mother, a small shell from the beach where he’d first took her to gaze at the horizon.

On stormy nights she set the box on the sill of the lamp room, facing out. The red eye caught the light of the lamp and held it. Ships passed, and the sea still lashed at the rock, and she felt his calming presence even during the height of the most violent storms. 

*Keep something in it you’d be sorry to lose. It will reside within and it will not sleep.*

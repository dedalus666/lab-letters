Moonlit Sky - Box 52

The ship was called Rocinante, after the horse of a man who charged at windmills, because he thought they were knights. Dulcinea chose the name on purpose. Everyone she’d ever told about her plan had said the same thing in different words: it was a dream, and a dream was a windmill. She decided to name her ship after the horse that carried the dreamer anyway.

Rocinante wasn’t beautiful. She was built from scrap: a boat hull, two old fuel tanks, a hundred pieces of other machines, red paint over the rust. Her only fin was a small red diamond, bolted on at the end like a flag. She had one engine, and it burned with a small, stubborn orange flame that didn’t look like it could carry anything higher than the roofline of a barn. 

The night of the launch, the neighbors came to watch, mostly to be polite. Dulcinea climbed in, closed the hatch, and said to the empty cabin, “Don’t make a fool out of me.”

The engine caught. The ground fell away. For a moment there was only noise, and then there was a silence so large it felt like a held breath, and she was above the clouds, and above the clouds the sky was black and the stars didn’t twinkle.

Ahead of her hung the moon, huge and gold, looking close enough to touch. And behind her, when she finally dared to turn, was the earth.

She hadn’t expected it. She’d spent so long looking up that she never imagined what it would be to look back. It was small, a blue and white marble, so delicate it seemed like something she could cup in her hands. All of it was in there: the kitchen where she’d been told no, the workshop, the neighbors, every windmill she’d ever been laughed at for charging.

She wasn’t sorry. She wasn’t proud, either. She simply felt, for the first time, the full, quiet enormity of what she’d done.

Rocinante’s little orange flame burned on against the dark, steady as a lamp. Somewhere below, a boy on a rooftop watched it climb until it was a point, and then a pinprick, and then a star among all the other stars.

Years later, a carpenter in a small town made a box as a tribute of that night. He gave it blue and teal for the sky, a gold moon in one corner, a blue-green earth below, the colored grain seperated the continents from the oceans, and a winged red rocket-ship with an orange spark at its tail, rising. He scored lines into the box, and scorched it too as representation of the trial by fire. The lid lifted off like a launch.

*Keep something in it you’d be sorry to lose. It will go with you, wherever you go.*

Skyward - Box 43

There was a harbor town where the weather never changed its mind. The sky hung over the roofs like wet wool, gray in the morning and grayer by night, and the people had stopped looking up. You can live a long time without noticing what you’ve stopped looking at.

A carpenter lived there, one of the quiet ones, who built chests for sailors. Scrap wood, mostly. Offcuts the shipyard threw away. One winter he started a chest that didn’t look like his others. He stained the lid in layers, blue over gold, and drew the clouds into it by letting the stain run where the grain would take it. Below the lid he set five diamonds in a row, teal and brown, their points together like the spread feathers of a bird mid-wing. On the very top he cut a slot, small and exact, and left it empty.

“What’s it for?” asked the girl who swept his floor.

“Fair weather,” he said. “It’s a chest for fair weather. But it only works if you put the sun in first.”

She laughed, because she thought he was joking. He wasn’t. A fair-weather chest, he told her, can’t make a good day. It can only keep one. You had to find a day worth keeping, and you had to carry it home yourself.

So she went looking. She tied a rope handle to each end of the chest, hauled it up the cliffs above the harbor, and waited. It took her three weeks. Then one morning a crack opened in the gray and bright blue appeared between the clouds, no wider than a blade, and a bird came through it, a swift or a hawk, she never knew, flying so high and so fast it seemed to be pulling the light up behind it. For a moment the whole sky behind the bird went red.

She held up the chest. The diamond in the slot caught that red like a coal catching a breath, and the groove closed around it. The lid shut with a sound like a door she’d never tried to open, but only stood outside of. 

When she opened the chest at home that night, the room was full of morning. It stayed that way for exactly as long as she needed it to. It was warm and nourishing light, good for the soul. 

The town never did clear up. But now and then, on a bad day, someone would knock at her door, and she’d lift the lid, and they’d stand there for a while in the light, saying nothing, looking up.

*Keep something in it you’d be sorry to lose. It will remember the sky.*

Octave - Box 44

In a town just east of Lyra, northwest of Pegasus, there was a musician who only knew one scale, the plain seven-step climb everyone learns first. You know the one; do-ra-me-fa-sol... This was not a handicap. There are twelve pitches in the world, and she had simply never wandered past the seven of that one scale. The trouble was that her songs never finished. She would sing up the scale, reach the seventh note, and stop. Something was missing, a door that never opened onto the next room.

She tried to end songs on the first note instead, and that sounded like going nowhere. She tried ending on the seventh, and that sounded like falling. So she carried her unfinished songs around like letters she’d never sent.

One night she met an old woodworker at the edge of the market, selling boxes out of a handcart. One of them was square, with a lid that slid off like a drawer. On it was a star with eight points, stained in purple, teal and dusty rose, and at the front was a small red diamond that served as a latch.

“Eight points,” she said. “I only know seven notes.”

“Count again,” the woodworker said. “Start at the bottom.”

She counted the points up one side of the star, seven, and then the eighth point was the first point again, but higher on the box. Same shape, same color, one level up.

“That’s the octave,” the woodworker said. “The eighth note isn’t new. It’s your first note, coming home, one level higher. But you aren’t who you were when you left it. You’ve climbed seven steps. So it sounds the same and means something else entirely.”

She slid the lid open and found it empty, and she understood that whatever she put inside would have to make the same trip. So she folded up the letter she’d carried longest, the oldest unfinished song, and put it in. Then she slid the lid closed, turned the red diamond a quarter turn until the latch caught, and went home.

That night she sang the seven notes, and where she had always stopped, she kept going. The eighth note came out of her like a door swinging open in a house she’d lived in all her life. It was her first note again, but higher, and everything the song had been through was inside it.

She finished that song, and then every song she’d ever left behind.

*Keep something in it you’d be sorry to lose. It will come back to you, one level higher.*

Aurora - Box 51

In the far north there is a winter that lasts for months. The sun goes down in autumn and does not come back until the ice begins to crack. In the village of Skarn, the people called it the Long Night, and they measured it in oil and in patience.

By the sixty-fifth day, the oil was low and the patience lower. The children had stopped asking when the sun would return. The old people had stopped saying.

A girl named Ilka, who was nine, went out onto the ice alone. She wasn’t looking for anything. She just wanted to be somewhere that wasn’t the inside of a dark house. She dragged a small wooden box behind her on a rope. The grain on the bottom was stained blue and red from being pulled over rocks of the same color. It was a simple, but curious box, the kind her father made out of scraps from the boatyard, with a latch on the front shaped like a butterfly and a handle of braided rope at each end. There were a number of odd geometric shapes on the lid, nobody knew what they represented. There was a red diamond in the middle, a ring of teal around it, and an array of diamond shapes spreading out from the center like the fingers of an open hand.

She sat on the ice, opened the lid, and said, to nobody, “When the sun comes back, I'm going to capture it.”

Far above her, the sky flickered.

A green line crossed the dark. Then a second, then a third, bending like cloth in wind. Red followed, low along the horizon, and violet, and for a moment the whole sky was a bird opening its wings from a single bright point. The light poured down across the ice until she could see her own shadow, long and blue, and the red diamond on her lid caught it and glowed like a coal.

It was like magic. Ilka had never been so small, or so held.

She stayed until the colors faded. Then she hauled the box home and showed everyone in Skarn where the sun had gone. It hadn’t left. It was just somewhere beyond the edge of the world, sending its light up in long bright feathers. It had sent a bird to remind them. That was what the odd shapes on the box represented. 

The old woman at the end of the village, who remembered ninety winters, put her hand on the painted lid and said, “That’s what dawn is. Not the sun itself, but a light in the sky as a signal. The first thing the sun does when it remembers us.”

Eleven days later, the sun returned. The village kept the box, and each Long Night someone opened it and watched the sky. 

*Keep something in it you’d be sorry to lose. It will glow when the dark is longest and be there when the light returns.*
